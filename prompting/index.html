Prompting 101

Stop treating Claude like a search engine. Start treating it like the world's most patient coworker.

⚡ In 30 seconds

The difference between a mediocre Claude response and a great one is almost always the prompt, not Claude. Give it context, a clear task, a format, and any constraints — and it'll knock it out of the park. This page shows you exactly how.

The golden rule

Talk to Claude like a smart new coworker, not a search engine.

A search engine wants keywords. Claude wants context. If you walked up to a sharp colleague on their first week and said "email supplier," they'd stare at you. Give them a situation, a goal, and what done looks like — and they'll run with it. Same deal here.

The more you treat Claude like a person you're briefing, the better the output. Full sentences. Real context. Clear ask. It's not more work — it's just different work, and it pays off fast.

The 4 ingredients of a great prompt

You don't need all four every time — but the more you include, the less back-and-forth you'll do.

1 · Context

What's the situation?

Who are you, what's going on, who is the audience? Claude can't read your mind — but it CAN read your prompt.

"I'm a manufacturing engineer at an EV startup writing to a parts supplier who's been slow on deliveries…"

2 · Task

What do you want?

Be specific. "Write an email" is vague. "Write a firm but polite follow-up email asking for a new delivery date" is a task.

"…write a firm but polite follow-up email asking for a committed delivery date by end of week."

3 · Format

How do you want it?

Email, bullet list, table, short paragraph, numbered steps? Claude defaults to whatever it thinks fits — tell it what you actually need.

"Format it as a professional email with a subject line."

4 · Constraints

Length, tone, audience?

Word limits, reading level, what to avoid, what to emphasize. Constraints aren't limits — they're shortcuts to the right answer.

"Keep it under 100 words. Don't sound passive-aggressive."

Weak → Strong: real Aptera examples

Same request. Completely different result.

Manufacturing

Weak

"Write an email to a supplier about late parts."

Strong

"I'm a manufacturing engineer at Aptera Motors. Our aluminum extrusion supplier (Acme Parts) is three weeks late on a critical door frame order that's holding up production. Write a professional but firm email asking them to commit to a delivery date by end of week. Keep it under 120 words. Tone: serious, not angry."

Finance

Weak

"Summarize this spreadsheet."

Strong

"I'm preparing for a Q3 budget review with the CFO. Here's our departmental spend data [paste data]. Summarize the top three budget variances, flag anything over 15% off target, and suggest two questions the CFO is likely to ask. Format as a bullet list, plain language — no jargon."

Marketing

Weak

"Write a social media post about our car."

Strong

"Write a LinkedIn post announcing that Aptera's solar-assisted EV can travel up to 40 miles a day on sunshine alone. Target audience: sustainability-minded professionals who aren't car people. Tone: exciting but grounded, no hype words like 'revolutionary' or 'game-changing.' Max 150 words. End with a question to drive comments."

The first answer is a draft

Nobody — not even the best prompt writers — gets a perfect result on the first try every time. That's not a bug, it's the workflow. Claude is designed for conversation, not one-shot queries.

After you get a first response, try any of these follow-ups:

  • "Make it shorter — aim for half the length."
  • "The tone is too formal. Make it sound like I actually wrote it."
  • "Give me three variations of the opening line."
  • "What's missing from this? What would you add?"

Three rounds of back-and-forth beats one heroic mega-prompt every time. Iterate like you'd polish a document with a smart editor — because that's basically what's happening. This habit has a name and a lesson of its own: Manual Looping.

Give it your stuff

Want the output to actually sound like you? Paste examples. Paste your own past emails, documents, or templates into the prompt. Say "write in this style" and hand it a sample. Claude is a fast learner — it'll mirror your voice, format, and vocabulary better than any setting or toggle.

Same goes for templates. Have a report format you always use? Drop it in and say "fill this in based on [data]." Let Claude do the formatting grunt work while you do the thinking.

Pro tip: start with the goal, not the format

Instead of "write me a table of X," try "I need to compare X across these five options — what's the best way to present this?" Claude will often suggest a format you hadn't considered, and it's usually better.

🧪 Your first strong prompt

Copy this template, fill in the brackets, and send it:

I'm a [your role] at Aptera Motors. [Describe the situation in 1–2 sentences.] I need you to [specific task]. Format it as [email / bullet list / table / short paragraph]. Keep it [under X words / concise / detailed]. Tone should be [professional / casual / direct / friendly]. Audience: [who will read this].

🔑 Key takeaways

  • Talk like you're briefing a smart coworker: Context + Task + Format + Constraints = great prompts.
  • Weak prompts get generic answers. One sentence of real context changes everything.
  • Iteration is the point — treat every first response as a rough draft and keep the conversation going.
